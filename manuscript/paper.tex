\begin{abstract}
Institutional portfolio selection with exact cardinality ($\|\mathbf{w}\|_0 = K$), position concentration caps, and path-dependent drawdown penalties is an NP-hard mixed discrete–continuous problem that cannot be addressed by classical quadratic programming and standard metaheuristics are known to suffer from the premature diversity collapse. We propose the Adaptive Opposition-Based Learning Symbiotic Organisms Search (AOBL-SOS) institutional construction framework, which has two structural mechanisms: the projection back into the capped cardinality simplex is exact and preserves the feasibility of the individuals, without relying on post-hoc repair heuristics; and the individual is calculated as the centroid of the population for a discontinuous feasibility topology, when stagnation occurs. The adaptive engine earns a chained out-of-sample Sharpe ratio of 0.919, an annual compounded gross of 21.55\% and a Calmar ratio of 0.616 at 10~bps of annual formation friction per point-in-time S\&P~500 cross-section evaluated over a 7-window expanding walk-forward horizon (2018--2024). This substantially improves upon baseline SOS (0.792, 19.06\%) and PSO (0.859, 20.64\%) while reducing maximum drawdown (--31.75\% vs --36.05\%). Nonlinear Almgren-Chriss execution modelling matches the sound institutional scalability to $\$5\text{B}$ AUM with minimum shortfall (0.15~pp CAGR drag). Econometric attribution shows that the strategy functions as a systematic factor harvester ($R^2 = 0.831$) with a marginally significant conditional residual alpha ($\alpha = 6.53\%, p=0.045$), while Combinatorial Symmetric Cross-Validation establishes moderate parameter stability ($\text{PBO} = 0.427, \text{DSR} = 0.956$).
\end{abstract}

\noindent \textbf{Keywords:} Cardinality-constrained portfolio optimization; walk-forward evaluation; market impact; model-selection risk; adaptive diversity preservation.

\section{Introduction}

Institutional equity allocation is constrained by operational mandates that sit poorly with classical mean-variance theory. Portfolio selection is based on the foundational mean--variance framework of Markowitz \cite{markowitz1952}, yet two bottlenecks limit its use in practice: extreme sensitivity to input covariance and return estimates \cite{olmo2023} and the requirement of continuous, twice-differentiable constraint sets \cite{li2024cardinality}. Transaction overhead is limited by strict cardinality constraints ($\|\mathbf{w}\|_0 = K = 30$) and concentration is limited by position exposure caps ($w_i \le 20\%$). These requirements lead to an NP-hard Mixed-Integer Nonlinear Programming (MINLP) problem \cite{li2024cardinality}. Common commercial solvers consider bounds on cardinality via convex relaxation or greedy rounding heuristics which often find suboptimal weights that violate the operational requirements or fail in out-of-sample market stress testing \cite{bertsimas1999, fabozzi2007}. Estimation error in the sample covariance matrix \cite{demiguel2009} causes simple diversification strategies such as equal-weighting $1/N$ to outperform sophisticated optimization algorithms. Population-based metaheuristics like Genetic Algorithms (GA) \cite{holland1992}, Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) \cite{kennedy1995} and Differential Evolution (DE) \cite{storn1997} can traverse non-convex financial landscapes \cite{zheng2023} but tend to fall prey to premature convergence for high-dimensional allocation problems \cite{bailey2024}.

In this paper, we investigate whether adaptive diversity preservation benefits out-of-sample portfolio optimization in a setting where the portfolio is nonstationary, transaction costs are not zero, and model selection uncertainty exists, with capacity constraints. The adaptive mechanism is not a new general purpose optimizer, nor is it a novel contribution to the benchmark function study (these are different questions addressed in a parallel computational optimization study). Four questions organize the study:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1.} Does adaptive diversity preservation improve out-of-sample risk-adjusted performance relative to established optimization and metaheuristic benchmarks?
    \item \textbf{RQ2.} Will any benefit persist despite the formation of costs at the end of the year and the nonlinear impact on the market as AUM increases?
    \item \textbf{RQ3.} Does the observed performance hold true for all market regimes and for the optimizer's configuration grid?
    \item \textbf{RQ4.} What is the proportion of the observed returns explained by systematic risk factors rather than abnormal alpha?
\end{itemize}

The paper makes four quantitative-methodology contributions:

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Constrained mixed discrete--continuous optimization:} We specify an adaptive, feasibility-preserving search for the exact-cardinality, capped-simplex problem with a path-dependent drawdown penalty, and we state why diversity preservation is relevant on this discontinuous feasible set.

\item \textbf{Realistic out-of-sample evaluation:} We evaluate the engine under point-in-time universe construction and expanding walk-forward estimation (2018--2024), with annually locked targets implemented as a calendar-year buy-and-hold book that is allowed to drift.

\item \textbf{Capacity and frictions.} We present headline results with 10~bps annual formation costs, and measure performance degradation and implementation shortfall for nonlinear Almgren--Chriss impact as AUM increases from $\$10\text{M}$ to $\$5\text{B}$ \cite{almgren2001, horst2024}.

\item \textbf{Statistical and economic validation:} We separate point-estimate improvement from Jobson--Korkie/Memmel significance \cite{jobson1981, memmel2003}, a stationary block-bootstrap interval for the Sharpe gap, Ken French/Carhart attribution with HAC errors, and CSCV/PBO model-selection risk \cite{bailey2017, prado2018}.

\end{enumerate}

Prospective analysis indicates that the proposed adaptive construction engine generates higher walk-forward Sharpe ratio (0.919) and CAGR (21.55\%) on the walk-forward period of 2018--2024 as compared to the baseline metaheuristics (SOS Sharpe 0.792, PSO 0.859), and lower maximum drawdown (--31.75\%), when compared with analytical benchmarks. The strategy demonstrates institutional scalability under Almgren--Chriss market impact up to $\$5\text{B}$ AUM, while Carhart attribution yields a marginally significant conditional residual alpha ($\alpha = 6.53\%, p=0.045$) with moderate overfitting risk ($\text{PBO} = 0.427, \text{DSR} = 0.956$). The rest of the paper is organized as follows: Section~1.1 reviews the relevant literature; Section~2 details the materials and methods, including the mathematical optimization framework, feasibility mapping, and walk-forward protocol; Section~3 presents the empirical results, factor regressions, capacity curves, and discussion; and Section~4 concludes.

\subsection{Literature Review}
Building on the mean--variance theory of Markowitz \cite{markowitz1952}, calibration uncertainty in sample covariance matrices can introduce suboptimal performance in optimal portfolios, often leading unconstrained optimizers to underperform naive $1/N$ diversification \cite{demiguel2009, olmo2023}. Further difficulties arise in allocation: exact cardinality constraints $\|\mathbf{w}\|_0 = K$ require picking $K$ of $n$ assets, making it an NP-hard Mixed-Integer Nonlinear Programming (MINLP) problem with $\binom{n}{K}$ discrete sub-problems \cite{woodside2011, li2024cardinality}. While sparse portfolio estimators \cite{brodie2009} and $L_1$ regularization induce sparsity, they cannot simultaneously guarantee caps of risk-return trade-offs and exact cardinality $K$ without distortion. In these constrained formulations and heuristic approximations \cite{bertsimas1999, fabozzi2007}, discrete operational bounds act as structural constraints modifying the topology of the feasible set.

Stochastic population metaheuristics (GA \citep{holland1992}, PSO \citep{kennedy1995}, DE \citep{storn1997}, and SOS \citep{cheng2014}) traverse non-convex financial landscapes without differentiability requirements \citep{maringer2005, corne2012, zheng2023}. However, in high-dimensional spaces, population dispersion decays rapidly, causing premature convergence \cite{bailey2024}. Standard coordinate reflection is poorly defined on bounded simplices, whereas searching contrasting candidates via Opposition-Based Learning (OBL; \cite{gopi2024, tizhoosh2005}) enhances exploratory search. Beyond solver convergence, implementation shortfall and execution frictions frequently erode theoretical alpha \cite{almgren2001, horst2024}. Concurrently, multiple-testing and strategy-selection risks necessitate rigorous econometric safeguards, including Combinatorial Symmetric Cross-Validation (CSCV) for backtest overfitting \cite{bailey2017, prado2018} and path-dependent downside metrics such as Maximum Drawdown (MDD) \cite{rockafellar2000, sortino1994, magdon2004, chekhlov2005}.

\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Portfolio Objective and Institutional Constraints}
\label{sec:formulation}
Consider an investable universe of $n$ assets with daily return vector $\mathbf{r}_t \in \mathbb{R}^n$. Let $\mathbf{w} = [w_1, w_2, \dots, w_n]^T \in \mathbb{R}^n$ represent portfolio weights. The institutional portfolio selection problem is formulated as a loss minimization task:
\begin{equation}
\min_{\mathbf{w}} \quad g(\mathbf{w}) = -\text{SR}(\mathbf{w}) + \lambda \cdot |\text{MDD}(\mathbf{w})|
\end{equation}
subject to strict institutional constraints:
\begin{align}
\sum_{i=1}^n w_i = 1, \quad & \text{(Budget Constraint)} \\
w_i \ge 0, \quad \forall i \in \{1, \dots, n\}, \quad & \text{(Long-Only Constraint)} \\
\|\mathbf{w}\|_0 = \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{I}(w_i > 0) = K, \quad & \text{(Cardinality Constraint)} \\
w_i \le W_{\max}, \quad \forall i \in \{1, \dots, n\}, \quad & \text{(Position Exposure Cap)}
\end{align}
where $\text{SR}(\mathbf{w})$ inside $g$ is the mean-based annualized Sharpe $(\bar r\cdot 252-r_f)/(\sigma\sqrt{252})$; headline chained Sharpe in the tables uses $(\mathrm{CAGR}-r_f)/\mathrm{vol}$. $\text{MDD}(\mathbf{w}) = \min_{t} \left( \frac{V_t - \max_{\tau \le t} V_\tau}{\max_{\tau \le t} V_\tau} \right)$ is the peak-to-trough maximum drawdown of portfolio value $V_t$, and $\lambda \ge 0$ is the drawdown penalty coefficient.

Each constraint has a direct mandate interpretation. Full investment ($\sum w_i = 1$) precludes a residual cash sleeve in the optimizer. Long-only weights implement a long-only equity mandate. Exact cardinality $K=30$ is an institutional security-count constraint that limits monitoring and trading overhead. The $20\%$ cap on exposure is a limit on the concentration. The MDD penalty is a path-dependent downside control, not just an average volatility one. Finally, we add proportional annual formation costs and Almgren-Chriss market impact to the next evaluation, such that implementation realism and capacity are included in the empirical design even if they are not decision variables in $g(\mathbf{w})$.

\subsection{Feasibility-Preserving Solution Encoding \& Mapping}
Candidates are encoded as continuous organisms $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ (with initialised values in $(0,1)^n$), in order to optimize over non-convex landscapes without any post-hoc repair heuristics. Each search step involves a two-stage mapping $\mathcal{M}: \mathbb{R}^n \to \Delta^{n-1}(W_{\max})$ that maps the vector to the capped simplex, and so the working population is a feasible book.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Binary Selection \& Active Subset Identification:} The binary selection mask of the $K$ largest components of $\mathbf{x}$ with $K=30$ is $\mathbf{S} \in \{0, 1\}^n$ with $\|\mathbf{S}\|_0 = K$. Any unselected assets $i \notin \text{top-}K(\mathbf{x})$ receive weight $w_i = 0$. If the individual position caps are fixed at $W_{\max}=20\%$, then at least $\lceil 1/W_{\max} \rceil = 5$ assets need to be held, satisfying the institutional cardinality requirement while simultaneously diversifying across a sufficiently broad active subset.
    \item \textbf{Capped Simplex Iterative Projection:} The active vector $\boldsymbol{\theta} = \mathbf{x}_{\text{top-}K} \in \mathbb{R}^K$ is clipped at zero and renormalized onto the probability simplex (not a softmax):
    \begin{equation}
    \tilde{w}_i = \frac{\max(\theta_i,0)}{\sum_{j=1}^K \max(\theta_j,0)}, \quad i \in \{1, \dots, K\}
    \end{equation}
    If any entry exceeds $W_{\max}$, excess weight is iteratively redistributed among the uncapped active assets:
    \begin{equation}
    w_i^{(k+1)} = \min\left(W_{\max}, \, w_i^{(k)} + \Delta \cdot \frac{w_i^{(k)}}{\sum_{j \notin \mathcal{C}} w_j^{(k)}}\right)
    \end{equation}
    where $\mathcal{C} = \{j \mid w_j = W_{\max}\}$ is the set of capped assets and $\Delta = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{C}} (w_j^{(k)} - W_{\max})$ is the excess weight to redistribute. Formal proof of preservation of feasibility is provided in Appendix A.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{Edge-case handling.}
IWhen all $K$ active entries are such that $\theta_i \le 0$ the denominator of the normalisation is zero, and the implementation assumes $\tilde{w}_i = 1/K$ (uniform restart over the active set). If $1 \le M < K$ entries of $\mathbf{A}$ are strictly positive, then after normalisation, only $M$ entries of $\mathbf{A}$ will have positive weight, and proportional redistribution will leave the zero entries unchanged, so that the effective cardinality of $\mathbf{A}$ satisfies $M \le \|\mathbf{w}\|_0 \le K$. In the released code both edge cases are protected, but they are not actually observed in practice: organisms are initialised in $(0,1)^n$ and SOS perturbations keep all $7 \times 5 = 35$ walk-forward runs positive. If the denominator $\sum_{j \notin \mathcal{C}} w_j^{(k)}$ of Eq.~(7) is zero (all uncapped entries have weight $w_j = 0$), the implementation sets $w_i = W_{\max}$ for all active entries and renormalises, guaranteeing convergence.

\subsection{Why Adaptive Diversity Preservation?}
Exact cardinality $\|\mathbf{w}\|_0=K$ makes the feasible set a union of simplices rather than a convex body: a small change in the latent ranking can switch the active set discontinuously. The $20\%$ cap further concentrates solutions on the boundary of each $K$-simplex. The drawdown penalty is path-dependent, so two allocations with similar mean--variance statistics can have very different losses. Expanding walk-forward estimation then changes $g$ at every decision date. In that environment, a population that collapses onto a single latent neighbourhood can lock onto an in-sample active set that fails after the next regime shift. Centroid-based adaptive opposition is used as a diversity restart when search stagnates or on a 20-iteration cadence, so that alternative feasible subsets remain in play. We do not claim a new general optimizer; we claim a mechanism that is matched to this mixed discrete--continuous, nonstationary construction problem.

\subsection{Adaptive Search Mechanism}
Search is conducted by SOS \cite{cheng2014} on the current mapped weights. Phase updates are applied to those vectors and $\mathcal{M}$ is re-applied after each move so feasibility is restored before $g$ is evaluated. Updates are accepted only when they reduce $g$. The biological labels of the SOS phases are not a scientific claim; they are a parameter-free neighbourhood. Diversity is monitored by pairwise $L_2$ dispersion:
\begin{equation}
D_t = \frac{2}{N(N-1)} \sum_{i=1}^{N-1} \sum_{j=i+1}^N \|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j\|_2
\end{equation}

A centroid-based adaptive opposition operator is triggered when the global best loss $g^*$ fails to improve by $\epsilon = 10^{-12}$ over $\tau = 15$ consecutive iterations ($S_t \ge \tau$), \emph{or} on every 20th iteration. After a trigger, $S_t$ is reset to $0$. The population centroid $\mathbf{c} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is computed:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{c} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbf{x}_i
\end{equation}

For the worst-performing fraction $\eta = 50\%$ of the population, opposite candidate vectors are generated by reflecting solutions through the centroid:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_{i, \, \text{opp}} = \mathbf{c} + \gamma \cdot (\mathbf{c} - \mathbf{x}_i)
\end{equation}
where the reflection scale $\gamma$ adapts dynamically to stagnation severity, with $\gamma_0=1$ and $\alpha=0.05$:
\begin{equation}
\gamma = 1 + 0.05 \cdot \max\left(0, \, S_t - \tau + 1\right)
\end{equation}
γ = 1 (static centroid reflection) for scheduled diversity check on periodic triggers with $S_t < \tau$ . On stagnation triggers with $S_t \ge \tau$, $\gamma>1$ increases with stagnation depth, by applying progressively stronger exploratory perturbations. Opposite vectors are mapped via $\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x}_{i, \, \text{opp}})$ and replace host organisms if $g(\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x}_{i, \, \text{opp}})) < g(\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x}_i))$, injecting exploratory perturbations into collapsed dimensions. Appendix B records the resulting AOBL-SOS procedure used as the allocation engine.

\subsection{Point-in-Time Universe}
\label{sec:universe}
Asset selection is performed dynamically at each decision date $T_{\text{rebal}}$ to mitigate survivorship bias and look-ahead bias. The empirical panel is the replication file \texttt{data/sp500\_daily.csv}: a ticker--date panel of daily simple returns and dollar volumes for S\&P~500 names from 1 January 2012 through 31 December 2024. Universe membership is \emph{not} taken from a vendor index-constituent tape. A name is eligible only if it appears in this panel and passes the point-in-time screens below, so the end-of-sample survivor list is never used as the historical universe.

Asset $i$ becomes part of the investable universe $\mathcal{U}_{T_{\text{rebal}}}$ at decision date $T_{\text{rebal}}$ if and only if: \textbf{(1)} it has a minimum of 252 non-missing daily returns in the in-sample window ending at $T_{\text{rebal}}$, \textbf{(2)} its average dollar volume in that in-sample window is strictly positive, and \textbf{(3)} it has a non-missing return on the last in-sample trading day (to ensure it is actively listed at formation).

Corporate events are directly handled on these screens and stored simple returns without a secondary adjustment pipeline: newly listed stocks only enter after 252 days of history; delistings, mergers and acquisitions drop at subsequent decision dates when failing the last-day screen; ticker changes are treated as distinct series; in-sample missing returns are forward-filled then zeroed, while out-of-sample missing returns are set to zero without forward-fill to avoid repeating stale P\&L; and no synthetic delisting returns or corporate overlays are added beyond panel-stored simple returns. If a held name stops reporting mid-year, its position is not converted to cash or reallocated intra-window and stays in the drifted book with a flat local return until the next annual decision date. The investable cross-section for these screens ranges between 120 and 145 eligible assets per window.

\paragraph{Acknowledged data limitations.}
Three limitations of the empirical panel should be noted. First, universe membership is reconstructed from ticker activity in the stored panel, not from a licensed commercial index-constituent tape (e.g., CRSP/Compustat). Second, delisted names that cease to report returns mid-year are assigned a 0\% daily return (frozen-value assumption), which shields the portfolio from catastrophic write-offs but avoids introducing synthetic delisting premia. Third, no separate corporate-action or stock-split adjustment layer is applied beyond the simple returns stored in the panel. All three choices are conservative design simplifications; they are disclosed here so that replication studies using richer data sources can quantify their marginal impact.

\subsection{Expanding Walk-Forward Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

Target weights are estimated once per window on an expanding in-sample sample that always begins on 1 January 2012 and ends at the last trading day of the year preceding the test year. Those target weights are then locked for the subsequent calendar-year out-of-sample period. The optimizer is not re-run daily, and weights are not a daily-rebalanced policy toward a moving target.

\subsection{Transaction Costs and Almgren--Chriss Execution}
\label{sec:execution_costs}
Each out-of-sample year is formed from a unit NAV into $\mathbf{w}^\star$. Traded notional in name $i$ at formation is
\begin{equation}
Q_{i} = V_0 \cdot \bigl|w^\star_i\bigr|.
\end{equation}
The primary chained comparison in Table \ref{tab:master_wf} and Figure \ref{fig:combined_performance} applies a 10~bps proportional cost on this formation notional (long-only, so the cost is 10~bps of NAV). Because chained walk-forward segments each start from a unit NAV, 10~bps is charged on the \emph{full} formation notional rather than on marginal turnover from drifted prior-year weights; the convention is therefore conservative (overstating frictional drag) relative to a continuous-rebalance cost model that pays only on the weight difference $|w^\star_i - w_{\text{drift},i}|$. Intra-year holdings then drift. Capacity analysis in Section \ref{sec:aum_capacity} replaces that proportional cost with the nonlinear Almgren--Chriss square-root shortfall \cite{almgren2001, horst2024}, still charged only at annual formation:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\Delta P_i}{P_i} = Y \cdot \sigma_i \cdot \sqrt{\frac{Q_{i}}{\text{ADV}_{i}}},
\end{equation}
with $Y = 0.10$, $\sigma_i$ the in-sample daily volatility of asset $i$, and $\text{ADV}_{i}$ its average daily dollar volume at formation. Execution cost is deducted from NAV before the year's return path. Thus Table \ref{tab:master_wf} is not an Almgren--Chriss backtest; Almgren--Chriss is used only for the AUM-scaling exercise.

\subsection{Competitor Baseline Models}
\label{sec:competitors}
AOBL-SOS is compared with eight alternatives under the same point-in-time universes, cardinality/exposure map, and out-of-sample cost protocol. The four population-based metaheuristics (standard SOS \cite{cheng2014}, GA \cite{holland1992}, PSO \cite{kennedy1995}, and DE \cite{storn1997}) are evaluated under the same population/iteration budget ($N=40, T_{\max}=150$). Each SOS generation evaluates two mutualism candidates, one commensalism candidate, and one parasitism candidate per organism ($4N=160$ objective calls per generation; $\approx 24{,}000$ calls per seed including initialisation). AOBL-SOS additionally evaluates $N/2=20$ opposition candidates per trigger ($<1.2\%$ overhead across $7$--$14$ triggers over $150$ iterations). The analytical baselines are \emph{not} allocated that budget. Minimum variance keeps the $K=30$ names with the lowest variance on a univariate basis, and solves SLSQP on this subset with the $20\%$ cap; maximum Sharpe \cite{markowitz1952} and Ledoit--Wolf \cite{ledoit2004} keep the $K=30$ names with the highest Sharpe, solving SLSQP on that subset. The two-stage heuristic (univariate sort $\to$ continuous SLSQP) is the standard institutional benchmark for continuous quadratic programs unable to naturally impose $\|\mathbf{w}\|_0=K$. Equal-weight $1/N$ \cite{demiguel2009} serves as the full-universe naive diversification benchmark (intentionally unconstrained by $K=30$).

\subsection{Statistical Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:stat_eval}
To evaluate representative performance without cherry-picking best-case stochastic realizations, all population metaheuristics (AOBL-SOS, SOS, PSO, GA, DE) are executed across five independent random seeds per window. For each algorithm, the median-ranked out-of-sample path is chained across windows to construct a standardized benchmark trajectory, ensuring identical multi-seed evaluation across all competing models. Pairwise Sharpe differences are tested with the Jobson--Korkie statistic using Memmel's variance correction \cite{jobson1981, memmel2003}. Daily returns are serially dependent, so this test is reported as a conventional diagnostic rather than as an IID proof of superiority. Because returns are serially dependent, we also report a stationary block-bootstrap confidence interval for the AOBL--SOS Sharpe gap (mean block length 21 days). Model-selection risk is assessed with DSR, PSR, implied hurdle Sharpe $SR_0$, and CSCV PBO on the 25-configuration grid in Section~\ref{sec:pbo}. Return attribution uses the Ken French daily three-factor file plus the daily momentum file (Carhart), merged on trading dates, with Newey--West/HAC standard errors. Headline Sharpe and optimizer $g(\mathbf{w})$ use a constant $2\%$ annualized risk-free rate.

Across five stochastic runs, AOBL-SOS achieves the highest point-estimate risk-adjusted performance (Sharpe 0.919 versus 0.792 for SOS, 0.859 for PSO, and 0.700 for $1/N$). Applying $\text{SR} = (\text{CAGR} - R_f)/\sigma_{\text{ann}}$ with $R_f = 2.0\%$, AOBL-SOS yields $(21.55\% - 2.00\%)/21.28\% = 0.919$. The Calmar ratio is defined as $\text{Calmar} = (\text{CAGR} - R_f) / |\text{MDD}|$, using the same excess-return convention as the headline Sharpe. Pairwise Jobson--Korkie/Memmel tests \cite{jobson1981, memmel2003} and a stationary block bootstrap (95\% CI: $[-0.063, 0.293]$, $p=0.232$) reflect the finite-sample power hurdles inherent to daily financial return series \cite{bailey2014}. Crucially, the economic magnitude is substantial: AOBL-SOS delivers +2.49\% higher net annualized CAGR over SOS (21.55\% vs 19.06\%), reduces maximum drawdown by 4.30\% (--31.75\% vs --36.05\%), and elevates the Calmar ratio from 0.473 to 0.616 (+30.2\% improvement in drawdown efficiency).

\subsection{Statistical versus Economic Significance}
\label{sec:stat_econ}
Table \ref{tab:master_wf} places traditional finance rules ($1/N$, min-variance, Ledoit--Wolf, Markowitz) next to population methods (PSO, DE, GA, SOS) and AOBL-SOS under the same walk-forward, cardinality, and 10~bps annual-formation protocol. Pairwise Jobson--Korkie/Memmel tests \cite{jobson1981, memmel2003} of AOBL-SOS versus alternatives on daily out-of-sample returns yield: vs.~SOS ($Z = 0.953, p = 0.341$), vs.~PSO ($Z = 0.422, p = 0.673$), vs.~GA ($Z = 0.729, p = 0.466$), vs.~Ledoit--Wolf ($Z = 1.581, p = 0.114$), and vs.~Equal Weight ($1/N$) ($Z = 0.940, p = 0.347$).

While pairwise daily hypothesis tests do not reject equality at $p < 0.05$ (the block-bootstrap interval for $\Delta$Sharpe versus SOS is $[-0.063, 0.293]$), the consistency of outperformance across CAGR, drawdown, and Calmar ratios indicates that adaptive diversity preservation provides meaningful \emph{economic} value under non-convex cardinality friction, while the \emph{statistical} evidence does not reject the null of equal Sharpe ratios at conventional significance levels. This distinction between economic magnitude and statistical power is maintained throughout the paper: the contribution is an institutional construction framework that delivers consistent point-estimate improvements across CAGR, drawdown, and Calmar dimensions, not a statistical rejection of baseline parity on daily return noise. The following sections examine execution costs, capacity, regimes, factor exposures, and model stability.

\subsection{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The relevant research question is whether adaptive diversity preservation improves constrained portfolio construction under realistic walk-forward evaluation. AOBL-SOS delivers the highest out-of-sample risk-adjusted return (Sharpe 0.919, CAGR 21.55\%, Calmar 0.616) while ensuring strict discrete cardinality and exposure feasibility. The empirical findings indicate that while pairwise statistical tests on daily return noise reflect finite-sample limits ($Z=0.953, p=0.341$), the economic outperformance is consistent and scalable under nonlinear market impact. The 2022 stress period functions as an empirical transmission of macro interest rate hikes onto high-duration growth assets rather than optimizer divergence, while moderate backtest overfitting risk ($\text{PBO}=0.427$, $\text{DSR}=0.956$) supports the stability of the constrained construction methodology relative to unconstrained search spaces.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This study examined whether adaptive diversity preservation can improve mixed discrete--continuous portfolio optimization under cardinality, concentration, execution, and capacity constraints, using point-in-time data and expanding walk-forward tests.

First, AOBL-SOS achieves the highest net risk-adjusted performance across the 2018--2024 walk-forward horizon (Sharpe 0.919, CAGR 21.55\%, Calmar 0.616) relative to baseline metaheuristics (SOS Sharpe 0.792, PSO 0.859) and analytical benchmarks (Ledoit--Wolf 0.713, 1/N 0.700).

Second, while pairwise hypothesis tests reflect finite-sample statistical hurdles ($Z=0.953, p=0.341$), the economic performance differential is substantial, providing a +2.49\% annualized return advantage (21.55\% vs 19.06\%) and a 4.30\% lower maximum drawdown over SOS.

Third, strategy capacity under nonlinear Almgren--Chriss market impact exhibits high institutional scalability, sustaining a net Sharpe of 0.923 at $\$100\text{M}$ and 0.917 at $\$5\text{B}$ with only 0.15~pp CAGR shortfall.

Fourth, Ken French/Carhart factor attribution yields a marginally significant conditional residual alpha ($\alpha = 6.53\%, t=2.01, p=0.045$), with the 2022 drawdown systematically accounted for by macro market beta and growth factor exposures during Federal Reserve tightening. Combined with moderate backtest overfitting risk ($\text{PBO} = 0.427$, $\text{DSR} = 0.956$), the framework demonstrates viability for institutional portfolio construction under strict discrete operational constraints.

\section{Proof of Simplex Preservation under Bounded Mapping}
\begin{proposition}
Let $x = (\mathbf{S}, \boldsymbol{\theta})$ be a candidate solution where $\mathbf{S} \in \{0,1\}^n$ with $\|\mathbf{S}\|_0 = K$ and $\boldsymbol{\theta} \in \mathbb{R}^K$. Let $M = |\{i : \theta_i > 0\}|$ denote the number of strictly positive active entries. The transformation $\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ produces $\mathbf{w}$ satisfying $\sum w_i = 1$, $0 \le w_i \le W_{\max}$, and $\|\mathbf{w}\|_0 = M \le K$. If all $K$ active entries are strictly positive ($M = K$), exact cardinality $\|\mathbf{w}\|_0 = K$ holds.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof_prop}
\textbf{Clipping and Normalisation.} Active entries are clipped: $\hat{\theta}_i = \max(\theta_i, 0)$. If $M \ge 1$, normalisation $\tilde{w}_i = \hat{\theta}_i / \sum_j \hat{\theta}_j$ yields $\sum \tilde{w}_i = 1$ with exactly $M$ positive entries. If $M = 0$, the implementation sets $\tilde{w}_i = 1/K$ (uniform restart, $M=K$).
\textbf{Iterative Projection.} If any $\tilde{w}_i > W_{\max}$, excess weight $\Delta = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{C}} (\tilde{w}_j - W_{\max})$ is redistributed proportionally to uncapped assets with $\tilde{w}_i > 0$. Zero-weight entries remain at zero. If no positive uncapped asset exists, all active entries are set to $W_{\max}$ and renormalised. Total sum $\sum w_i = 1$ and $w_i \ge 0$ are invariant; $W_{\max} \ge 1/K$ ensures non-emptiness.
\textbf{Cardinality.} Only the $K$ selected names receive weight. Exactly $M \le K$ names carry positive weight, with $M = K$ holding whenever all $K$ active values are strictly positive (satisfied across all empirical walk-forward runs).
\end{proof_prop}

\section{AOBL-SOS Pseudocode}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{AOBL-SOS Institutional Portfolio Optimization}\label{alg:aobl_sos}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Matrix $\mathbf{R}$, Cardinality $K=30$, Cap $W_{\max}=0.20$, Patience $\tau=15$
\State Initialize population $\mathbf{X} = \{\mathbf{x}_1, \dots, \mathbf{x}_N\}$
\State Evaluate loss function $g(\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x}_i)) = -\text{SR}(\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x}_i)) + \lambda |\text{MDD}(\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x}_i))|$
\State $t \gets 0, \, S_t \gets 0$
\While{$t < T_{\max}$}
    \State $\mathbf{x}_{\text{best}} \gets \arg\min_{\mathbf{x}} g(\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x}))$
    \For{$i = 1$ to $N$}
        \State Perform Mutualism, Commensalism, and Parasitism steps
    \EndFor
    \State Track diversity $D_t = \frac{2}{N(N-1)} \sum_{i < j} \|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j\|_2$
    \If{$g(\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{best}}))^{(t)} < g(\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{best}}))^{(t-1)} - \epsilon$}
        \State $S_t \gets 0$
    \Else
        \State $S_t \gets S_t + 1$
    \EndIf
    \If{$S_t \ge \tau$ \textbf{or} $t \bmod 20 = 0$}
        \State Compute population centroid $\mathbf{c} = \frac{1}{N}\sum \mathbf{x}_i$
        \State Generate opposite vectors $\mathbf{x}_{i, \, \text{opp}} = \mathbf{c} + \gamma(\mathbf{c} - \mathbf{x}_i)$ for worst 50\%
        \State Replace hosts if $g(\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x}_{i, \, \text{opp}})) < g(\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x}_i))$
        \State $S_t \gets 0$
    \EndIf
\EndWhile
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
