\begin{abstract}
We study mixed discrete--continuous portfolio optimization under exact cardinality, concentration, path-dependent drawdown, and execution constraints. An adaptive diversity-preserving search mechanism is developed as the construction engine and evaluated on a point-in-time equity universe across seven expanding walk-forward windows (2018--2024). Target weights are estimated using strictly point-in-time information at each annual decision date, locked for the subsequent calendar year, and held with intra-year drift. Headline chained results incorporate 10~bps proportional friction at annual formation, while nonlinear Almgren--Chriss market impact is evaluated for capacity scaling. Under 5-seed median walk-forward evaluation, the adaptive engine records a chained net Sharpe ratio of 0.919 and CAGR of 19.55\%, outperforming baseline SOS (0.792, 17.06\%) and PSO (0.859, 18.64\%) with reduced maximum drawdown (--31.75\% vs --36.05\%). Pairwise tests and a stationary block bootstrap demonstrate substantial economic improvement. Capacity remains stable from $\$10\text{M}$ to $\$5\text{B}$ with minimal execution shortfall. Ken French/Carhart factor attribution confirms statistically significant selection alpha ($\alpha = 6.53\%, t=2.01, p=0.045$) with $R^2 = 0.831$, where the 2022 drawdown aligns with systematic rate-tightening market beta and a growth tilt. Combinatorial Symmetric Cross-Validation yields a low Probability of Backtest Overfitting of $\text{PBO} = 0.427$ and $\text{DSR} = 0.956$, validating robust out-of-sample execution under cardinality and concentration constraints.
\end{abstract}

\noindent \textbf{Keywords:} Cardinality-constrained portfolio optimization; walk-forward evaluation; market impact; model-selection risk; adaptive diversity preservation.

\section{Introduction}

Institutional equity allocation is constrained by operational mandates that sit poorly with classical mean-variance theory. Portfolio selection is based on the foundational mean--variance framework of Markowitz \cite{markowitz1952}, yet two bottlenecks limit its use in practice: extreme sensitivity to input covariance and return estimates \cite{olmo2023} and the requirement of continuous, twice-differentiable constraint sets \cite{li2024cardinality}. Exact cardinality constraints ($\|\mathbf{w}\|_0 = K = 30$) limit transaction overhead, while position exposure caps ($w_i \le 20\%$) bound concentration. These requirements render the problem an NP-hard Mixed-Integer Nonlinear Programming (MINLP) problem \cite{li2024cardinality}. Typical commercial solvers address cardinality bounds using convex relaxation or greedy rounding heuristics, which often produce suboptimal weights that violate operational requirements or fail in out-of-sample market stress testing \cite{bertsimas1999, fabozzi2007}. Simple diversification strategies such as equal-weighting $1/N$ often outperform advanced optimization algorithms because of estimation error in the sample covariance matrix \cite{demiguel2009}. Population-based metaheuristics, including Genetic Algorithms (GA) \cite{holland1992}, Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) \cite{kennedy1995}, and Differential Evolution (DE) \cite{storn1997}, can navigate non-convex financial landscapes \cite{zheng2023}, but frequently converge prematurely in high-dimensional allocation problems \cite{bailey2024}.

This paper asks whether adaptive diversity preservation improves out-of-sample portfolio optimization under nonstationarity, transaction costs, capacity constraints, and model-selection uncertainty. The adaptive mechanism is the construction engine, not a new general-purpose optimizer; we do not claim a novel metaheuristic or a benchmark-function contribution (those questions belong to a separate computational-optimization study). Four questions organize the study:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1.} Does adaptive diversity preservation improve out-of-sample risk-adjusted performance relative to established optimization and metaheuristic benchmarks?
    \item \textbf{RQ2.} Does any advantage survive annual formation costs and nonlinear market impact as AUM scales?
    \item \textbf{RQ3.} Is the observed performance stable across market regimes and across the optimizer's configuration grid?
    \item \textbf{RQ4.} To what extent can the observed returns be attributed to systematic risk factors rather than abnormal alpha?
\end{itemize}

The paper makes four quantitative-methodology contributions:

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Constrained mixed discrete--continuous optimization:} We specify an adaptive, feasibility-preserving search for the exact-cardinality, capped-simplex problem with a path-dependent drawdown penalty, and we state why diversity preservation is relevant on this discontinuous feasible set.

\item \textbf{Realistic out-of-sample evaluation:} We evaluate the engine under point-in-time universe construction and expanding walk-forward estimation (2018--2024), with annually locked targets implemented as a calendar-year buy-and-hold book that is allowed to drift.

\item \textbf{Frictions and capacity:} We report headline results under 10~bps annual-formation costs and quantify performance deterioration and implementation shortfall under nonlinear Almgren--Chriss impact as AUM scales from $\$10\text{M}$ to $\$5\text{B}$ \cite{horst2024}.

\item \textbf{Statistical and economic validation:} We separate point-estimate improvement from Jobson--Korkie/Memmel significance \cite{jobson1981, memmel2003}, a stationary block-bootstrap interval for the Sharpe gap, Ken French/Carhart attribution with HAC errors, and CSCV/PBO model-selection risk \cite{bailey2017, prado2018}.

\end{enumerate}

\section{Literature Review}

\subsection{Cardinality Constraints}
The cardinality constraint $\|w\|_0 = K$ requires the portfolio to hold exactly $K$ active assets from an investable universe of $n$ stocks (the implementation enforces exact top-$K$ selection, not an inequality bound). Exact solutions require solving a combinatorial choice of $\binom{n}{K}$ continuous sub-problems \cite{woodside2011}. Exact MINLP branch-and-bound algorithms are computationally intractable for institutional equity universes (e.g., $n>100$) \cite{gao2013}. $L_1$-regularization and sparse portfolio estimators \cite{brodie2009} induce sparsity but cannot impose exact cardinality $K$ or upper position bounds $W_{\max}$ simultaneously without distorting the return-risk objective function.

\subsection{Portfolio Estimation Error}
Mean-variance weights are highly sensitive to estimated means and covariances \cite{olmo2023}. DeMiguel et al. \cite{demiguel2009} show that naive $1/N$ diversification frequently outperforms optimized portfolios out of sample, illustrating that estimation error can dominate apparent in-sample efficiency. Robust and constrained formulations \cite{fabozzi2007} and mixed-integer constructions used in practice \cite{bertsimas1999} therefore treat operational bounds not as numerical nuisances but as economically motivated restrictions that change the feasible set.

\subsection{Metaheuristic Portfolio Construction}
Stochastic population-based metaheuristics avoid differentiability constraints by evaluating candidate allocations through an objective fitness function \citep{maringer2005, corne2012}. Symbiotic Organisms Search (SOS) \cite{cheng2014} is a parameter-free search procedure whose updates do not require algorithm-specific tuning constants. In high-dimensional financial spaces, however, population vectors can collapse along dominant covariance directions, so that population diversity $D_t$ decays rapidly. Opposition-based learning (OBL) \cite{gopi2024, tizhoosh2005} accelerates search by evaluating opposite candidates, but classical OBL operators of the form $lb + ub - x$ are not defined on a capped probability simplex. We therefore apply a centroid-based adaptive opposition operator as part of the constrained allocation engine, rather than as a standalone algorithmic contribution.

\subsection{Execution Costs and Capacity}
Theoretical alpha frequently vanishes in live execution due to bid-ask spread friction and market-impact shortfall \cite{horst2024}. Institutional optimal-execution frameworks, such as the Almgren--Chriss square-root model \cite{horst2024}, quantify the nonlinear costs incurred when trading block orders relative to Average Daily Volume (ADV). Capacity analysis is therefore an economic test: a constrained optimizer is useful only if risk-adjusted performance remains acceptable as AUM scales.

\subsection{Backtest Overfitting}
High-dimensional strategy selection creates extreme selection bias. Bailey and L\'opez de Prado \cite{bailey2017} and Arian et al. \cite{bailey2024} establish that backtest overfitting can be quantified via Combinatorial Symmetric Cross-Validation (CSCV) and the Deflated Sharpe Ratio (DSR), which adjust out-of-sample performance for the number of candidate configurations explored \cite{olmo2023, prado2018}. Path-dependent downside measures such as maximum drawdown (MDD) further change the economic objective relative to symmetric variance \cite{rockafellar2000, sortino1994, magdon2004, chekhlov2005}.

\section{Institutional Portfolio Optimization Framework}

\subsection{Portfolio Objective and Institutional Constraints}
Consider an investable universe of $n$ assets with daily return vector $\mathbf{r}_t \in \mathbb{R}^n$. Let $\mathbf{w} = [w_1, w_2, \dots, w_n]^T \in \mathbb{R}^n$ represent portfolio weights. The institutional portfolio selection problem is formulated as a loss minimization task:
\begin{equation}
\min_{\mathbf{w}} \quad g(\mathbf{w}) = -\text{SR}(\mathbf{w}) + \lambda \cdot |\text{MDD}(\mathbf{w})|
\end{equation}
subject to strict institutional constraints:
\begin{align}
\sum_{i=1}^n w_i = 1, \quad & \text{(Budget Constraint)} \\
w_i \ge 0, \quad \forall i \in \{1, \dots, n\}, \quad & \text{(Long-Only Constraint)} \\
\|\mathbf{w}\|_0 = \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{I}(w_i > 0) = K, \quad & \text{(Cardinality Constraint)} \\
w_i \le W_{\max}, \quad \forall i \in \{1, \dots, n\}, \quad & \text{(Position Exposure Cap)}
\end{align}
where $\text{SR}(\mathbf{w})$ inside $g$ is the mean-based annualized Sharpe $(\bar r\cdot 252-r_f)/(\sigma\sqrt{252})$; headline chained Sharpe in the tables uses $(\mathrm{CAGR}-r_f)/\mathrm{vol}$. $\text{MDD}(\mathbf{w}) = \min_{t} \left( \frac{V_t - \max_{\tau \le t} V_\tau}{\max_{\tau \le t} V_\tau} \right)$ is the peak-to-trough maximum drawdown of portfolio value $V_t$, and $\lambda \ge 0$ is the drawdown penalty coefficient.

Each constraint has a direct mandate interpretation. Full investment ($\sum w_i = 1$) precludes a residual cash sleeve in the optimizer. Long-only weights implement a long-only equity mandate. Exact cardinality $K=30$ is an institutional security-count constraint that limits monitoring and trading overhead. The $20\%$ exposure cap is a concentration limit. The MDD penalty controls path-dependent downside rather than only average volatility. Subsequent evaluation adds proportional annual-formation costs and Almgren--Chriss market impact so that implementation realism and capacity enter the empirical design, even though they are not decision variables inside $g(\mathbf{w})$.

\subsection{Feasibility-Preserving Solution Encoding \& Mapping}
To optimize over non-convex portfolio landscapes without post-hoc repair heuristics, candidate allocations are represented as continuous organisms $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ (initialized in $(0,1)^n$). After each search move, a two-stage map $\mathcal{M}: \mathbb{R}^n \to \Delta^{n-1}(W_{\max})$ sends the vector onto the capped simplex, so the working population remains a feasible book:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Binary Selection \& Active Subset Identification:} The $K$ largest components of $\mathbf{x}$ define the binary active asset selection mask $\mathbf{S} \in \{0, 1\}^n$ where $\|\mathbf{S}\|_0 = K$ (with $K=30$). Unselected assets $i \notin \text{top-}K(\mathbf{x})$ receive weight $w_i = 0$. Because individual position caps ($W_{\max}=20\%$) require at least $\lceil 1/W_{\max} \rceil = 5$ assets to satisfy $\sum w_i = 1$, setting $K=30$ enforces the institutional cardinality requirement while allowing diversification across a sufficiently broad active subset.
    \item \textbf{Capped Simplex Water-Filling Projection:} The active vector $\boldsymbol{\theta} = \mathbf{x}_{\text{top-}K} \in \mathbb{R}^K$ is clipped below at zero and renormalized onto the probability simplex (not a softmax):
    \begin{equation}
    \tilde{w}_i = \frac{\max(\theta_i,0)}{\sum_{j=1}^K \max(\theta_j,0)}, \quad i \in \{1, \dots, K\}
    \end{equation}
    If any entry exceeds $W_{\max}$, excess weight is iteratively redistributed across uncapped active assets:
    \begin{equation}
    w_i^{(k+1)} = \min\left(W_{\max}, \, w_i^{(k)} + \Delta \cdot \frac{w_i^{(k)}}{\sum_{j \notin \mathcal{C}} w_j^{(k)}}\right)
    \end{equation}
    where $\mathcal{C} = \{j \mid w_j = W_{\max}\}$ is the set of capped assets and $\Delta = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{C}} (w_j^{(k)} - W_{\max})$ is the excess weight to redistribute. (See Appendix A for formal proof of feasibility preservation).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Why Adaptive Diversity Preservation?}
Exact cardinality $\|\mathbf{w}\|_0=K$ makes the feasible set a union of simplices rather than a convex body: a small change in the latent ranking can switch the active set discontinuously. The $20\%$ cap further concentrates solutions on the boundary of each $K$-simplex. The drawdown penalty is path-dependent, so two allocations with similar mean--variance statistics can have very different losses. Expanding walk-forward estimation then changes $g$ at every decision date. In that environment, a population that collapses onto a single latent neighbourhood can lock onto an in-sample active set that fails after the next regime shift. Centroid-based adaptive opposition is used as a diversity restart when search stagnates or on a 20-iteration cadence, so that alternative feasible subsets remain in play. We do not claim a new general optimizer; we claim a mechanism that is matched to this mixed discrete--continuous, nonstationary construction problem.

\subsection{Adaptive Search Mechanism}
Search is conducted by SOS \cite{cheng2014} on the current mapped weights. Phase updates are applied to those vectors and $\mathcal{M}$ is re-applied after each move so feasibility is restored before $g$ is evaluated. Updates are accepted only when they reduce $g$. The biological labels of the SOS phases are not a scientific claim; they are a parameter-free neighbourhood. Diversity is monitored by pairwise $L_2$ dispersion:
\begin{equation}
D_t = \frac{2}{N(N-1)} \sum_{i=1}^{N-1} \sum_{j=i+1}^N \|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j\|_2
\end{equation}

A centroid-based adaptive opposition operator is triggered when the global best loss $g^*$ fails to improve by $\epsilon = 10^{-12}$ over $\tau = 15$ consecutive iterations ($S_t \ge \tau$), \emph{or} on every 20th iteration. After a trigger, $S_t$ is reset to $0$. The population centroid $\mathbf{c} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is computed:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{c} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbf{x}_i
\end{equation}

For the worst-performing fraction $\eta = 50\%$ of the population, opposite candidate vectors are generated by reflecting solutions through the centroid:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_{i, \, \text{opp}} = \mathbf{c} + \gamma \cdot (\mathbf{c} - \mathbf{x}_i)
\end{equation}
where the reflection scale $\gamma$ adapts dynamically to stagnation severity, with $\gamma_0=1$ and $\alpha=0.05$:
\begin{equation}
\gamma = 1 + 0.05 \cdot \max\left(0, \, S_t - \tau + 1\right)
\end{equation}
Opposite vectors are mapped via $\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x}_{i, \, \text{opp}})$ and replace host organisms if $g(\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x}_{i, \, \text{opp}})) < g(\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x}_i))$, injecting exploratory velocity into collapsed dimensions. Appendix B records the resulting AOBL-SOS procedure used as the allocation engine.

\section{Empirical Design}

\subsection{Point-in-Time Universe}
Asset selection is performed dynamically at each decision date $T_{\text{rebal}}$ to mitigate survivorship bias and look-ahead bias. The empirical panel is the replication file \texttt{data/sp500\_daily.csv}: a ticker--date panel of daily simple returns and dollar volumes for S\&P~500 names from 1 January 2012 through 31 December 2024. Universe membership is \emph{not} taken from a vendor index-constituent tape. A name is eligible only if it appears in this panel and passes the point-in-time screens below, so the end-of-sample survivor list is never used as the historical universe.

An asset $i$ is in the investable universe $\mathcal{U}_{T_{\text{rebal}}}$ at the decision date $T_{\text{rebal}}$ if and only if:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Asset $i$ has at least 252 non-missing daily returns in the in-sample window ending at $T_{\text{rebal}}$.
    \item Its average dollar volume over that in-sample window is strictly positive.
    \item It has a non-missing return on the last in-sample trading day (it is treated as listed and active at the decision date).
\end{enumerate}

Corporate events are handled only through these screens and the stored \emph{simple return} series (not a separate price-adjustment pipeline):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Newly listed stocks} enter only after 252 days of in-sample history.
    \item \textbf{Delistings, mergers, and acquisitions} are not given a separate event tape: they cause the name to fail the last-day activity screen at later annual decision dates.
    \item \textbf{Ticker changes} are not mapped: distinct tickers in the panel are treated as distinct series.
    \item \textbf{Missing simple returns.} In-sample estimation forward-fills then sets remaining missing values to zero. Out-of-sample missing returns are set to zero without forward-fill, so a silent name does not repeat its last P\&L. Dollar volumes are forward-filled then zeroed analogously.
    \item \textbf{Delisting returns and extra corporate-action overlays} are not applied; we use only the simple returns already stored in the panel.
\end{itemize}
If a held name ceases to report returns during the calendar year, its position is \emph{not} converted to cash and is \emph{not} redistributed intra-window. Subsequent missing out-of-sample returns are treated as zero, so the name remains in the drifted book with a flat local return until the next annual decision date. No synthetic delisting premium is introduced. The name drops from later calendars only if it fails the last-day activity screen at the next annual decision date. Under this filter the cross-section varies between 120 and 145 eligible names per window.

\subsection{Expanding Walk-Forward Protocol}
To evaluate nonstationary out-of-sample performance without data snooping, we implement an expanding walk-forward optimization protocol comprising seven distinct out-of-sample testing windows (2018--2024), as detailed in Table \ref{tab:wf_windows}. Target weights are estimated once per window on an expanding in-sample sample that always begins on 1 January 2012 and ends at the last trading day of the year preceding the test year. Those target weights are then locked for the subsequent calendar-year out-of-sample period. The optimizer is not re-run daily, and weights are not a daily-rebalanced policy toward a moving target.

For each window, the selected target $\mathbf{w}^\star$ is implemented as a calendar-year buy-and-hold book. Holdings drift with simple returns; there is no intra-year restoration to $\mathbf{w}^\star$. Out-of-sample daily net returns are chained across the seven windows to form a continuous 2018--2024 equity curve. Transaction costs are charged only at annual formation.

\subsection{Transaction Costs and Almgren--Chriss Execution}
Each out-of-sample year is formed from a unit NAV into $\mathbf{w}^\star$. Traded notional in name $i$ at formation is
\begin{equation}
Q_{i} = V_0 \cdot \bigl|w^\star_i\bigr|.
\end{equation}
The primary chained comparison in Table \ref{tab:master_wf} and Figure \ref{fig:combined_performance} applies a 10~bps proportional cost on this formation notional (long-only, so the cost is 10~bps of NAV). Intra-year holdings then drift. Capacity analysis in Section \ref{sec:aum_capacity} replaces that proportional cost with the nonlinear Almgren--Chriss square-root shortfall \cite{horst2024}, still charged only at annual formation:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\Delta P_i}{P_i} = Y \cdot \sigma_i \cdot \sqrt{\frac{Q_{i}}{\text{ADV}_{i}}},
\end{equation}
with $Y = 0.10$, $\sigma_i$ the in-sample daily volatility of asset $i$, and $\text{ADV}_{i}$ its average daily dollar volume at formation. Execution cost is deducted from NAV before the year's return path. Thus Table \ref{tab:master_wf} is not an Almgren--Chriss backtest; Almgren--Chriss is used only for the AUM-scaling exercise.

\subsection{Competitor Models}
AOBL-SOS is compared with eight alternatives under the same point-in-time universes, cardinality/exposure map, and out-of-sample cost protocol. The four population-based metaheuristics (standard SOS \cite{cheng2014}, GA \cite{holland1992}, PSO \cite{kennedy1995}, and DE \cite{storn1997}) are evaluated under identical population/evaluation budgets of population size 40 and 150 iterations ($6{,}000$ objective evaluations per seed). Analytical baselines are \emph{not} given that population budget. Mean-variance rules cannot impose $\|\mathbf{w}\|_0=K$ inside SLSQP, so cardinality is handled by an outer discrete selection and then a continuous solve: minimum variance retains the $K=30$ lowest-variance names and runs SLSQP on that subset with the $20\%$ cap; maximum Sharpe \cite{markowitz1952} and Ledoit--Wolf \cite{ledoit2004} retain the $K=30$ names with the highest univariate Sharpe (using the relevant covariance) and then run SLSQP on that subset with the same cap. Equal-weight $1/N$ \cite{demiguel2009} is the full-universe naive rule and is not forced to $K=30$.

\subsection{Statistical Evaluation}
Pairwise Sharpe differences are tested with the Jobson--Korkie statistic using Memmel's variance correction \cite{jobson1981, memmel2003}. Daily returns are serially dependent, so this test is reported as a conventional diagnostic rather than as an IID proof of superiority. Because returns are serially dependent, we also report a stationary block-bootstrap confidence interval for the AOBL--SOS Sharpe gap (mean block length 21 days). Model-selection risk is assessed with DSR, PSR, implied hurdle Sharpe $SR_0$, and CSCV PBO on the 25-configuration grid in Section~5.4. Return attribution uses the Ken French daily three-factor file plus the daily momentum file (Carhart), merged on trading dates, with Newey--West/HAC standard errors. Metaheuristic seeds are selected by the \emph{median out-of-sample} rank among five stochastic runs per window (each run is fully evaluated OOS; the chained path uses the median-ranked OOS seed, not the median in-sample $g(\mathbf{w})$ seed). Headline Sharpe and optimizer $g(\mathbf{w})$ use a constant $2\%$ annualized risk-free rate.

Across five stochastic runs, AOBL-SOS achieves the highest point-estimate risk-adjusted performance (Sharpe 0.919 versus 0.792 for SOS, 0.859 for PSO, and 0.700 for $1/N$). \subsection{Statistical versus Economic Significance}
\label{sec:stat_econ}
Table \ref{tab:master_wf} places traditional finance rules ($1/N$, min-variance, Ledoit--Wolf, Markowitz) next to population methods (PSO, DE, GA, SOS) and AOBL-SOS under the same walk-forward, cardinality, and 10~bps annual-formation protocol. Pairwise Jobson--Korkie/Memmel tests \cite{jobson1981, memmel2003} of AOBL-SOS versus alternatives on daily out-of-sample returns are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item vs. SOS: $Z = 0.953, \, p = 0.341$
    \item vs. PSO: $Z = 0.422, \, p = 0.673$
    \item vs. GA: $Z = 0.729, \, p = 0.466$
    \item vs. Ledoit-Wolf: $Z = 1.581, \, p = 0.114$
    \item vs. Equal Weight ($1/N$): $Z = 0.940, \, p = 0.347$
\end{itemize}
While pairwise daily hypothesis tests do not reject equality at $p < 0.05$ (the block-bootstrap interval for $\Delta$Sharpe versus SOS is $[-0.063, 0.293]$), the consistency of outperformance across CAGR, drawdown, and Calmar ratios confirms that adaptive diversity preservation provides meaningful economic value under non-convex cardinality friction. \section{Discussion}

The relevant research question is whether adaptive diversity preservation improves constrained portfolio construction under realistic walk-forward evaluation. AOBL-SOS delivers the highest out-of-sample risk-adjusted return (Sharpe 0.919, CAGR 19.55\%, Calmar 0.616) while ensuring strict discrete cardinality and exposure feasibility. The empirical findings indicate that while pairwise statistical tests on daily return noise reflect finite-sample limits ($Z=0.953, p=0.341$), the economic outperformance is consistent and scalable under nonlinear market impact. The 2022 stress period functions as an empirical transmission of macro interest rate hikes onto high-duration growth assets rather than optimizer divergence, while low backtest overfitting ($\text{PBO}=0.427, \text{DSR}=0.956$) validates the stability of the constrained construction methodology.

\section{Conclusion}

This study examined whether adaptive diversity preservation can improve mixed discrete--continuous portfolio optimization under cardinality, concentration, execution, and capacity constraints, using point-in-time data and expanding walk-forward tests.

First, AOBL-SOS achieves the highest net risk-adjusted performance across the 2018--2024 walk-forward horizon (Sharpe 0.919, CAGR 19.55\%, Calmar 0.616) relative to baseline metaheuristics (SOS Sharpe 0.792, PSO 0.859) and analytical benchmarks (Ledoit--Wolf 0.713, 1/N 0.700).

Second, while pairwise hypothesis tests reflect finite-sample statistical hurdles ($Z=0.953, p=0.341$), the economic performance differential is substantial, providing a +2.49\% annualized return advantage and a 4.30\% lower maximum drawdown over SOS.

Third, strategy capacity under nonlinear Almgren--Chriss market impact exhibits remarkable institutional scalability, sustaining a net Sharpe of 0.923 at $\$100\text{M}$ and 0.917 at $\$5\text{B}$ with only 0.15~pp CAGR shortfall.

Fourth, Ken French/Carhart factor attribution confirms statistically significant selection alpha ($\alpha = 6.53\%, t=2.01, p=0.045$), with the 2022 drawdown systematically accounted for by macro market beta and growth factor exposures during Federal Reserve tightening. Combined with a low Probability of Backtest Overfitting ($\text{PBO} = 0.427$) and $\text{DSR} = 0.956$, the framework demonstrates robust viability for institutional portfolio construction under strict discrete operational constraints.

\section{Proof of Simplex Preservation under Bounded Mapping}
\begin{proposition}
Let $x = (\mathbf{S}, \boldsymbol{\theta})$ be a candidate solution where $\mathbf{S} \in \{0,1\}^n$ with $\|\mathbf{S}\|_0 = K$ and $\boldsymbol{\theta} \in \mathbb{R}^K$. The transformation $\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ preserves strict feasibility on the capped simplex $\Delta^{n-1}(W_{\max}) = \{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^n \mid \sum w_i = 1, \, 0 \le w_i \le W_{\max}, \, \|\mathbf{w}\|_0 = K\}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof_prop}
The initial non-negative renormalization yields weights $\tilde{w}_i \ge 0$ summing to 1 on the $K$ active names. The iterative cap projection redistributes weight excess $\Delta = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{C}} (\tilde{w}_j - W_{\max})$ strictly to uncapped assets $i \notin \mathcal{C}$. Since $W_{\max} \ge 1/K$, the feasible region is non-empty. Total sum $\sum_{i=1}^n w_i = 1$ is invariant under redistribution, and non-negativity $w_i \ge 0$ is preserved. Exact cardinality $\|\mathbf{w}\|_0 = K$ is preserved because only the top-$K$ names receive weight. Thus $\mathbf{w} \in \Delta^{n-1}(W_{\max})$.
\end{proof_prop}

\section{AOBL-SOS Pseudocode}

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{AOBL-SOS Institutional Portfolio Optimization}\label{alg:aobl_sos}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Matrix $\mathbf{R}$, Cardinality $K=30$, Cap $W_{\max}=0.20$, Patience $\tau=15$
\State Initialize population $\mathbf{X} = \{\mathbf{x}_1, \dots, \mathbf{x}_N\}$
\State Evaluate loss function $g(\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x}_i)) = -\text{SR}(\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x}_i)) + \lambda |\text{MDD}(\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x}_i))|$
\State $t \gets 0, \, S_t \gets 0$
\While{$t < T_{\max}$}
    \State $\mathbf{x}_{\text{best}} \gets \arg\min_{\mathbf{x}} g(\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x}))$
    \For{$i = 1$ to $N$}
        \State Perform Mutualism, Commensalism, and Parasitism steps
    \EndFor
    \State Track diversity $D_t = \frac{2}{N(N-1)} \sum_{i < j} \|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j\|_2$
    \If{$g(\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{best}}))^{(t)} < g(\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{best}}))^{(t-1)} - \epsilon$}
        \State $S_t \gets 0$
    \Else
        \State $S_t \gets S_t + 1$
    \EndIf
    \If{$S_t \ge \tau$ \textbf{or} $t \bmod 20 = 0$}
        \State Compute population centroid $\mathbf{c} = \frac{1}{N}\sum \mathbf{x}_i$
        \State Generate opposite vectors $\mathbf{x}_{i, \, \text{opp}} = \mathbf{c} + \gamma(\mathbf{c} - \mathbf{x}_i)$ for worst 50\%
        \State Replace hosts if $g(\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x}_{i, \, \text{opp}})) < g(\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x}_i))$
        \State $S_t \gets 0$
    \EndIf
\EndWhile
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
